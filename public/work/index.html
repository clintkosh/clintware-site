Check Point / Avanan example

One job family showed the full range better than a title alone can.

My Check Point / Avanan work combined a directly owned Customer Success portfolio with a much broader shared operating layer across Harmony Email & Collaboration customers. That meant strategic cadence and executive ownership on one side, and high-volume day-to-day customer operations on the other.

Direct customer ownership

Strategic book with recurring cadence

I managed a rotating portfolio commonly described in my resume as roughly 20–40 SaaS customers representing about $3M in account value, ranging from smaller accounts through multi-million-dollar strategic relationships. The work included onboarding, adoption, telemetry and health reviews, Customer Success Plans, QBRs and EBRs, executive alignment, technical risk, escalations, renewal readiness, and growth discovery.

Scaled operating coverage

The wider customer base still needed someone watching the system

I also became a primary operating point for the HEC customer distribution-list motion, covering onboarding, customer and Sales questions, licensing clarification, portal and policy issues, escalation routing, renewal support, and post-sale requests. That shared layer served a much wider population, including smaller customers and customers without a dedicated CSM, while the organization worked to maintain dedicated coverage for larger accounts.

The invisible work

A calm account can be the output of substantial work upstream.

Some of my most useful work never becomes a customer-visible event. It is risk caught before a renewal surprise, a technical issue routed before it becomes an executive escalation, an internal disagreement resolved before it creates conflicting guidance, or a recurring failure turned into a playbook before the next customer encounters it.

Prevention

The best escalation can be the one that never happens.

I watch for changes in support demand, usage, stakeholder participation, response cadence, unresolved commitments, technical friction, product fit, and business context. Those weak signals often tell the story before a red health score or a renewal date does.

Response

When the fire exists, create control quickly.

I establish impact, create one customer narrative, identify owners, separate immediate recovery from root-cause prevention, set a communication cadence, and keep the customer informed without pretending to be the engineer writing the fix.

How I operate

High-level patterns I reuse across roles.

Customer mission first

Capture why they bought, then keep it alive

Use the original objective as the basis for onboarding, success planning, business reviews, adoption evidence, risk decisions, renewal readiness, and expansion only when a real unmet need exists.

Signals before surprises

Look at change, not only static scores

Support history, usage movement, stakeholder changes, silence, missed commitments, tone, recurring friction, and renewal timing all become more useful when tied to an owner and a next action.

One narrative in a fire

Customer confidence comes from controlled communication

Impact, evidence, owner, next step, next update. Keep specialists focused on the fix while the customer gets one coherent path through the incident.

Standardize the common work

Save human judgment for the exceptions

Playbooks, templates, automation, clear handoffs, self-service, and reporting should remove repeated coordination without turning every customer into the same workflow.

Translate across functions

Business outcome ↔ technical reality

I can communicate with executives, CSMs, Sales, Support, Product, Engineering, and operations without pretending those disciplines are interchangeable.

Build what is missing

Do more than diagnose the operating gap

When the missing answer is a dashboard, playbook, workflow, prototype, automation, or working tool, I am comfortable building the first useful version and iterating from evidence.
