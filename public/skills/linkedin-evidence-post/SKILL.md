# LinkedIn Evidence Post

Turn a real observation into a LinkedIn post that sounds like a person who learned something, not a content template trying to manufacture authority.

The governing rule is:

> **OBSERVATION -> PROOF -> PRINCIPLE -> RELEVANCE -> STOP**

For interview or career-reflection posts, a useful compact variant is:

> **PRESENT -> PROOF -> FUTURE -> STOP**

The final step is intentional. A strong post does not need to answer every possible question. It should make the point clearly, leave one useful thread for the reader to pull, and end.

## Default workflow

1. **Identify the actual point.** Reduce the post to one sentence: what changed in the author's thinking, behavior, or operating method?
2. **Recover evidence before drafting.** Use supplied notes, metrics, feedback, transcripts, article context, or observed outcomes. Never invent a supporting anecdote or number.
3. **Choose one proof point.** Prefer one concrete metric, short example, or observed contrast over a stack of claims.
4. **Extract the principle.** State what the proof changed or clarified. Do not merely retell the event.
5. **Make the relevance explicit.** Connect the lesson to how the author now works, decides, prepares, leads, builds, or communicates.
6. **Stop before over-explaining.** Remove the sentence that tries to pre-answer every objection.
7. **Run the Devil's Advocate gate.** Read the post as a skeptical recruiter, hiring manager, customer, peer, executive, or prospective collaborator.
8. **Return the finished post in natural paragraphs.** Use isolated one-line text only when a framework, contrast, or closing line benefits from visual emphasis.

## When the post references an article

Treat the article as the spark, not the content.

Use this sequence:

**ARTICLE IDEA -> FIELD OBSERVATION -> PROOF -> OWN FRAMEWORK -> PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY**

The post should add something the article does not already say. A useful opening may simply establish alignment, but the body must quickly move to the author's own evidence or experience.

Do not:
- summarize the whole article;
- praise it for a paragraph before adding a point;
- copy distinctive wording from the source;
- present the source's conclusion as personal evidence;
- force a disagreement merely to sound original.

## The proof-breadcrumb rule

Use enough evidence to establish credibility, then leave room for the next question.

A proof point should behave like a breadcrumb: concrete enough to be believable, but bounded enough that a reader can naturally ask for the next layer.

This is especially useful when translating interview lessons into public writing. A concise answer or post often works better when it creates the next useful question instead of attempting to close every branch of the conversation.

## Preparation versus performance

When the lesson involves communication, interviews, presentations, or executive conversations, distinguish preparation from rehearsal.

The stronger operating pattern is:

- know the numbers;
- know the stories;
- know the audience;
- know the point;
- do not memorize the performance.

Preparation should increase confidence and retrieval speed. It should not make the delivery sound read, recited, or mechanically polished.

## Using vulnerability without creating a liability

A specific misstep can make a post credible, but only when it is framed as evidence of learning rather than an excuse.

If a line could make a reasonable reader infer poor judgment, weak preparation, over-preparation, desperation, defensiveness, or lack of spontaneity, immediately test whether the learning is clear.

Prefer:

**specific signal -> owned interpretation -> changed behavior**

Avoid:

**specific failure -> explanation -> defense**

An anonymized example is usually enough. Do not name a private contact, employer, customer, or active opportunity unless the user explicitly wants that information public and it is appropriate to publish.

## Audience awareness

Know who the post is for without turning the post into a disguised direct message.

If one specific person is likely to recognize the story:
- keep the post independently useful to the broader audience;
- do not tag them merely to force visibility;
- avoid language that calls them out or pressures them to respond;
- let the shared context be recognizable without making it the post's entire purpose.

The post should still work if that person never sees it.

## Human-cadence rules

Prefer:
- natural paragraphs;
- plain language;
- one central idea;
- specific verbs;
- one strong example;
- short frameworks when they genuinely clarify;
- an ending that sounds final.

Avoid:
- every sentence on its own line;
- repeated rhetorical questions;
- manufactured controversy;
- "agree?" or similar engagement bait;
- excessive hashtags;
- over-symmetrical sentence construction;
- generic thought-leadership language;
- stacked abstractions;
- a motivational closing that is unrelated to the evidence;
- polishing away the author's natural voice.

Preserve the user's established tone, punctuation, capitalization, and formatting preferences when known.

## Metrics and claims

Use a number only when its source and meaning are known.

A metric should answer at least one of these:
- What changed?
- By how much?
- Over what scope?
- Why does it matter?

Do not add a percentage merely because posts with numbers can look authoritative.

For current platform behavior, posting-time guidance, algorithm claims, feature availability, or other time-sensitive advice, verify current information rather than encoding a permanent "best time" into the skill.

## Devil's Advocate release gate

Before releasing the post, check all of the following:

1. **One-point test:** Can the main lesson be stated in one sentence?
2. **Evidence test:** Is there at least one grounded proof point when the claim requires proof?
3. **So-what test:** Does the reader understand what changed because of the observation?
4. **Human test:** Would a credible person actually write this, or does it sound generated?
5. **Risk test:** Could a reasonable target reader draw an unintended negative conclusion from a vulnerable detail?
6. **Article-value test:** If sharing an article, does the post add original field evidence or a useful framework?
7. **Breadcrumb test:** Did the post leave a natural opening for further conversation instead of exhausting every point?
8. **Brevity test:** Can any sentence be removed without losing evidence, meaning, or voice?
9. **Privacy test:** Does the post avoid exposing private people, pipelines, customers, or internal details without explicit approval?
10. **Stop test:** Does the post actually end once the point lands?

Any material failure requires revision.

## Practical structures

### Reflective professional post

**Observation -> one concrete example -> lesson -> changed operating behavior -> stop**

### Article reshare

**Why this aligns -> what the author observed independently -> one proof point -> author's practical framework -> stop**

### Evidence-led expertise post

**Problem -> proof -> operating principle -> implication for the reader -> stop**

### Interview-learning post

**Present -> Proof -> Future -> Stop**

For the last structure, "Future" is not a promise or aspiration paragraph. It is the concise statement of how the lesson changes the next conversation or decision.

## Final standard

A strong LinkedIn post should feel discovered rather than manufactured.

The target is not maximum polish. The target is credible evidence, a clear point, natural cadence, and enough restraint to let the reader participate in the conversation.
